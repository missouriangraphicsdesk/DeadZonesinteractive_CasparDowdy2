The journey from Missouri farms to gulf 'dead zones'

By Caspar Dowdy | May 12, 2026

Each year, Missouri's agriculture industry adds more nitrogen and phosphorus to the ground, spending trillions of dollars on chemical fertilizer. Hundreds of miles south, the ocean takes in those excess nutrients.

Fighting pollution at the source

As a major agricultural producer, Missouri is also a major contributor to nutrient pollution throughout the Mississippi River Basin.

The Department of Natural Resources uses the Nonpoint Source Management Plan to limit pollution caused by things like agricultural runoff in Missouri’s water bodies. This spring, the state’s NSMP underwent a routine review and update, which was submitted to the Environmental Protection Agency for approval in mid-April.

Throughout the midwest, nitrogen and phosphorus are added to farm fields by the ton.

Crops like corn absorb these nutrients through their roots and use them to stimulate growth.

Not all of the nutrients end up in a plant. As water runs over the ground, excess nitrogen and phosphorus are carried along with it.

Those nutrients eventually make it to larger water bodies, where they supercharge the growth of algae.

Those algae consume oxygen as they decay. if an algae bloom is large enough, it can cause the water body to become hypoxic*.

Hypoxia: low levels of dissolved oxygen in water. can cause “dead zones” devoid of aquatic life.

as hypoxia zones grow along the mississippi river system, midwest states face a challenge:

how can nutrient pollution be prevented at the source - without sacrificing productivity?

Fertilizer spending in the Hypoxia Task Force region

The twelve states in the Hypoxia Task Force are some of the most agriculturally productive regions in the world. In 2022, the region spent a combined $31 trillion on fertilizer inputs, representing 10.9 percent of farm operating costs on average. In 2019, fertilizer spending made up 9.3 percent of farm operating expenses on average.
